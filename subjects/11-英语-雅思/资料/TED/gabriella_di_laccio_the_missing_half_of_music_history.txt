(Singing)

This song is almost 900 years old. It was written by Comtessa de Dia, a poet and composer from medieval southern France. It is the only surviving melody written by a woman from that entire tradition. So let me start with the question: who decides which voices survive? I never really thought about that until a few years ago, when I was working in a second-hand book market in London, and I found an encyclopedia that listed 6,000 women composers in the history of music. I could not believe it because at that time I think I could name five. But as soon as I started looking into their names and listening to this incredible music, I kid you not, I would not shut up. Well I haven’t shut up. I’m here. And I just am sharing this music with everybody all the time. In fact, please allow me.

(Music)

I mean, can you believe that? Did you hear all the classical strings? The Afro-American dances mixed with the romantic music? Well welcome to my husband’s life in 2018.

(Laughter)

Now, my husband, he’s a railway engineer, which means he's now the railway engineer who knows the most about women composers in the whole world.

(Laughter)

I know, it's not a very competitive field, but still.

(Laughter)

So one day he said to me, "Look, it's very nice that you're playing this music by these women at breakfast for like, I don't know, a year now. But you do understand that nothing is going to happen if you just tell me. I'm an engineer. So are you going to do something or what?" And I was like, "Fair enough." So I did. I made a list of these incredible women. I put it online, and within months, women from all over the world started writing to me and they said, "Thank you so much for what you're doing. I'm a composer. Could you please add me to your list?" And I tell you, this list continues to grow. It is now a foundation, and we are now uncovering over 18,000 women composers in the history of music.

(Applause)

Now this story makes absolutely no sense. I'm a professional musician. How is it possible that I spent my life training and performing without hearing about thousands of women who wrote beautiful music? Well when I started doing my own research, and I came across the data, then it became clear because, it turns out, that nearly 90 percent of the music we hear across genres is written by men. That's a very big gap. Can you imagine all the amazing music we've been all missing all this time? But also that AI is now learning from that same silence. On one platform only, more than 50,000 AI-generated tracks are added every single day. The systems can only learn from the data available to them, but right now that data is missing. Half of humanity's creativity. Now I believe musicians should be writing music, not machines.

(Applause)

There are millions of talented people who can do that. Writing music fills our soul ... as humans. And to generate music from artists' work without their consent or proper remuneration is wrong.

(Applause and cheers)

But the technology is here to stay. So let's make sure all voices are heard, right? So how do we do that? It's actually quite easy. We must record the missing voices -- the historical ones, but also the current ones who are not represented. Because a recording makes this music searchable, teachable, and part of the systems shaping what the world will hear next. It brings this music to classrooms, to radio stations, to streaming platforms so everybody in this room can play and share it. So here's my invitation. What if every tech company making AI music and anyone profiting from it would invest in making our musical catalog more complete? Can you imagine? What gets heard is chosen, and those choices will shape what the future will remember. So who decides which voices survive? Now more than ever, we do.

And I want to leave you all with one woman's story. Ilse Weber was a poet, a singer who was imprisoned in the ghetto of Theresienstadt during World War II with her family. As she was there, she didn't write symphonies, but she wrote songs to entertain the elderly and the children. That's the power of music. And that was the gift of one woman. She and her son sadly died in Auschwitz, but her husband buried her manuscripts under a tool shed, and because of that, a few of her songs survived. This one is a lullaby. It's called "Wiegala".
